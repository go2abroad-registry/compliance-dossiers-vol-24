Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

South African Revenue Service (SARS) (www.sars.gov.za); self-service digital channels: SARS eFiling (https://www.sarsefiling.co.za) for income tax/VAT/PAYE returns and the SARS Customs Modernisation electronic declaration portal (SAD500) for customs and excise; company incorporation cross-registration with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC).

E-government filing portal

For a South African-incorporated (Pty) Ltd, CIPC integrates incorporation with SARS: the company registration number is issued and an income tax reference number (IT TIN) is auto-created, after which the entity must register for VAT (VAT101 application), PAYE/UIF/SDL, Compensation Fund, and the Customs client code, and it must appoint a resident public officer (s27 Income Tax Act; TAA s246). VAT registration is compulsory when taxable supplies exceed or are reasonably expected to exceed ZAR 1,000,000 in any consecutive 12-month period (VAT Act s23), and voluntary registration is available once supplies exceed ZAR 50,000 in a 12-month period (s24). A foreign-incorporated enterprise not registered in South Africa must first obtain a SARS tax reference number by physically lodging at a SARS branch or through a registered tax practitioner, submitting certified copies of the foreign certificate of incorporation, memorandum/articles (or equivalent), board resolution appointing a local public officer/representative taxpayer, a local physical business address, and proof of the South African permanent establishment or enterprise activity (bunkering supply contracts, jetty/tanker charter, or storage licence); SARS will then issue the composite TIN and the applicant may proceed with VAT and customs registrations. All registrations and the taxpayer status pin may be completed on eFiling after initial biometric/TPN onboarding.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The South African rand (ZAR) is freely convertible for current-account trade and service settlements through an Authorised Dealer; the capital account is restricted under the Currency and Exchanges Act 9 of 1933 and the Exchange Control Regulations. Non-resident share capital may be introduced without prior SARB approval if routed through an Authorised Dealer, but every inward investment must be reported and recorded. Profit remittance is available as a current-account outflow once the underlying equity has been issued and the non-resident investor is recorded; the main hurdles are documentary compliance with the bank under the Financial Intelligence Centre Act 38 of 2001, proof of beneficial ownership, tax-residency certification of the shareholder, and payment/withholding of dividends tax at 15% (reduced under applicable double-taxation agreement only after a treaty relief application has been accepted by SARS). The bank will require board resolutions, solvency confirmation under the Companies Act 71 of 2008, and a completed inward/outward investment reporting schedule.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export receipts, including payments for ship bunkering, marine fuel, and lubricant supplies delivered in South African territory to vessels engaged in international trade, must be received in full in South Africa within 180 days from the date of export or rendering of service. FinSurv may extend this period to 365 days in exceptional circumstances upon written application. There is no fixed mandatory conversion ratio into ZAR; exporters may retain foreign currency in an Authorised Dealer-operated Foreign Currency Account (FCA) provided the proceeds are credited through the Authorised Dealer within the 180-day window and the FCA has been applied for in advance. For customs purposes, bunkering supplies are exports under the Customs and Excise Act 91 of 1964, requiring an EX1 declaration and a Bunker Delivery Note (BDN) to support the surrender of proceeds.

Central bank supervision: South African Reserve Bank (SARB) – Financial Surveillance Department (FinSurv)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low – A foreign digital EMI cannot provide a South African-local clearing account or act as an Authorised Dealer in foreign exchange. It may be used as a supplementary card-issuing or multi-currency collection tool, but it cannot hold ZAR or settle corporate treasury payments without a South African banking counterparty. The Banks Act 94 of 1990 reserves deposit-taking from the public for licensed banks, and an offshore EMI is not licensed by SARB to conduct such business in South Africa.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward capital contributions for equity must be routed through an Authorised Dealer (Tier-1 commercial bank). The remitting bank sends a SWIFT MT103 with remittance information clearly stating 'SHARE SUBSCRIPTION – INWARD INVESTMENT' and the non-resident entity's legal name. The receiving Authorised Dealer requests a board resolution authorising the share issuance, source-of-funds documentation from the investor, and a completed inward investment application; the dealer then allocates the SARB Balance of Payments purpose classification for direct investment equity capital. Funds may be held in the Capital Escrow Account until the share certificate is issued and the non-resident shareholding is endorsed. Once the inward investment is recorded, the escrow balance can be released into the company's operational account for lawful working capital.For payments to foreign bunker suppliers, lubricant traders, or port service providers, the South African company must apply to its Authorised Dealer for spot foreign exchange with the underlying commercial contract, invoice, Bunker Delivery Note, and customs documentation. The dealer debits the ZAR operational account, buys the foreign currency, and settles via SWIFT MT103. For larger capital-equipment or significant fuel-import contracts, a Commercial Letter of Credit (MT700) is used; for performance and supply guarantees, a Standby Letter of Credit (MT760) is the customary rail. All outbound payments are reported monthly by the Authorised Dealer to SARB FinSurv, and prepayment for imports is limited to the 90-day threshold unless prior FinSurv approval is obtained.Forward Exchange Contract (FEC) with a SARB Authorised Dealer, supported by an underlying USD/ZAR payable or receivable arising from physical bunker sales or international fuel purchases; maximum tenor of 12 months.Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) on USD/ZAR, permitted only where the company has a designated foreign-currency functional currency for the bunkering operation and the transaction is executed through a SARB-registered dealer with FinSurv notification; otherwise, NDFs are reserved for non-resident shareholders.Cross-Currency Swap (CCS) with an Authorised Dealer for hedging a long-term US dollar-denominated term loan provided by the non-resident parent, subject to FinSurv approval of the underlying foreign-loan facility and full documentation of interest and principal flows.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: ZAR 5,000,000 aggregate annual cross-border connected-party transactions (South Africa does not impose a fixed statutory threshold; SARS may require transfer pricing documentation, including Master File and Local File, where the aggregate value of affected transactions exceeds ZAR 5 million or on a risk-based audit selection).

Economic substance scope: South Africa does not have a separate 'Economic Substance Regulations' regime comparable to UAE or Cayman. However, the doctrine is applied through the Income Tax Act's s.80A-GAAR, s.31 transfer pricing rules, the 'place of effective management' residence test, and controlled foreign company (CFC) legislation. SARS will disregard or re-characterise a transaction or corporate form where commercial substance is absent. For bunkering entities, the presence of local operational control, management decisions and tanker/bunker supply capacity is essential.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Understatement

Statutory sanction: SARS ex parte assessment; understatement penalty of up to 200% of the resultant shortfall under s.222 of the Tax Administration Act; criminal prosecution under s.235 TAA: fine up to ZAR 2,000,000 and/or imprisonment not exceeding 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held jointly and severally responsible under the Companies Act 71 of 2008 (s.77(9)) for unlawful distributions or reckless trading; SARS may pierce the corporate veil where the Pty Ltd is used as a facade; travel bans and restriction orders are possible under s.183 TAA; imprisonment is a personal criminal consequence for directors who knowingly participated.
Shell Company / Absence of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: SARS re-characterisation of transactions, disallowing excessive deductions, invoking GAAR adjustments (s.80A-L), and applying transfer pricing penalties. Understatement penalties of 50%-200% may apply; the company's registration may be deregistered by CIPC.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal assessment where the corporate veil is pierced; liability for unpaid tax arises under common law and s.169(1)(b) of the Income Tax Act; disqualification from holding directorships and travel bans may be imposed; criminal prosecution for reckless trading under the Companies Act may follow.
AML / Trade-Based Money Laundering and Sanctions Evasion

Statutory sanction: Financial Intelligence Centre Act administrative penalties up to ZAR 10 million per violation; Customs and Excise Act penalties for false declarations: fine of up to 10 times the value of the goods and twice the duty evaded, plus imprisonment of up to 5 years; criminal asset forfeiture under POCA.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or negligently facilitate trade-based money laundering or sanctions violations are personally exposed to civil damages, joint and several penalty liability, asset forfeiture orders, travel bans, loss of professional licences, and imprisonment for up to 15 years under the Prevention of Organised Crime Act and the Protection of Constitutional Democracy Against Terrorist and Related Activities Act.
