Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

South African Revenue Service (SARS) — Unified tax, customs, and excise administration; digital portals: SARS eFiling (https://www.sarsefiling.co.za) for tax registration and returns, SARS eCustoms (SAD 500) for export declarations, and SARS e-invoicing API for electronic tax invoice clearance.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 — Incorporate the Proprietary Limited company with the Companies and Intellectual Properties Commission (CIPC) to obtain a company registration number. Step 2 — Register for income tax via SARS eFiling within 60 business days of becoming liable under the Tax Administration Act 28 of 2011 (s.38), obtaining the SARS income tax reference number (15-digit tax reference). Step 3 — Apply for VAT registration: compulsory where taxable turnover exceeds ZAR 1,000,000 in any 12-month period, with application within 21 days after the end of the month of threshold breach; voluntary registration is available once turnover exceeds ZAR 50,000. Filing is via SARS eFiling (forms VAT101/VAT102), and SARS issues a separate 10-digit VAT registration number. Step 4 — For export operations, register as an exporter/importer with SARS Customs to obtain a customs code and enable electronic SAD 500 declarations. Non-resident shareholders are permitted; a foreign company trading through a branch must register as an external company with CIPC and obtain a South African non-resident taxpayer reference number from SARS.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a resident (Pty) Ltd is taxed as a separate taxpayer on its worldwide income at the 27% corporate income tax rate (years of assessment ending on or after 31 March 2023); distributions are subject to 20% dividends tax (withholding); non-resident branches are taxed only on South African-source income; no Transparency/Pass-Through election is available for a (Pty) Ltd.

Available election forms

South Africa does not operate a US-style check-the-box election; classification is determined by the statutory 'company' definition under the Income Tax Act 58 of 1962. Available statutory elections relevant to cross-border commodity traders: (1) VAT group election under s.50 of the VAT Act — 100%-related resident vendors may elect a single VAT number and consolidated VAT201 returns; (2) functional currency election under Income Tax Act s.31D for foreign branch or controlled foreign company reporting; (3) headquarter company election under Income Tax Act s.9I (approval-based, requiring at least 80% of income derived from qualifying foreign sources and an annual economic substance threshold); (4) small-business turnover tax election under s.48A for companies with annual turnover ≤ ZAR 1 million (taxable at 0%-3% instead of CIT); (5) hybrid mismatch rules (s.8F–s.8L) are mandatory anti-avoidance provisions, not elections, and disallow deduction/non-inclusion benefits where cross-border arbitrage exists.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Compulsory registration: ZAR 1,000,000 taxable turnover in any 12-month period; voluntary registration: ZAR 50,000 turnover threshold (SARS VAT 404 Guide and VAT Act ss.23 and 25). A non-resident enterprise making taxable supplies in South Africa is registered once the same threshold is met; where no fixed place of business exists, registration is nonetheless required for import/export zero-rating refund claims.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

0% VAT applies under VAT Act s.11(1)(a) to the supply of movable goods (including ginned raw cotton bales) under a sale of goods where SARS is satisfied that the goods are exported to another country in the ordinary course of business, provided physical export occurs within 90 days after the time of supply (invoice date). The vendor must retain proof of export and must not use the goods in South Africa prior to export. Alternatively, s.11(1)(j) zero-rates services supplied to non-residents. A refund of attributable input VAT arises under VAT Act s.54 where output VAT is zero-rated and the vendor files a VAT201 return, subject to a common-law and anti-fraud verification process by SARS.

  • Customs export declaration on form SAD 500 lodged via SARS eCustoms with electronic release status and customs code
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical removal of goods from South Africa to the foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice, packing list and weight list matching the SAD 500 description, quantity, and value of raw cotton bales
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) issued by an authorised dealer (South African Reserve Bank — SARB) evidencing foreign-currency receipt of the export proceeds
  • Certificate of Origin (SACU / SADC / AfCFTA preferential certificate where claimed)
  • Sale contract or purchase order evidencing transfer of ownership to a non-resident buyer and the FOB/CIF export terms
  • SARS-reconciled proof-of-export package (including the VAT control codes generated on eFiling VAT201) retained for 5 years for audit purposes

No Latin American-style real-time fiscal clearance or compulsory digital stamping exists in South Africa. SARS has, however, commenced phased implementation of an OECD-aligned national e-invoicing framework for high-turnover vendors (from 2024 onward), requiring electronic tax invoices to be exchanged through SARS-recognised or API-integrated billing platforms. Every VAT tax invoice must state the 24 prescribed particulars per VAT Act s.20(4), including the SARS tax invoice serial number, vendor VAT number, purchaser VAT number (where above ZAR 5,000), price, VAT amount and rate. Simplified invoices (≤ ZAR 5,000) may omit purchaser details. VAT returns must be submitted via SARS eFiling (form VAT201) with immediate electronic validation acknowledgement; SARS Customs clearance of export declarations occurs through eCustoms rather than via a VAT-specific fiscal stamp.

If zero-rating is claimed without a valid SAD 500 release, transport documentation, or within the 90-day export window, SARS reclassifies the supply as standard-rated at 15% and issues an additional assessment for the VAT shortfall plus prescribed-rate interest. Under the Tax Administration Act 28 of 2011 (s.222), understatement penalties of 25% (substantial understatement), 50% (reasonable grounds but no substantial understatement), 75% (gross negligence), or 200% (intentional not disclosed) apply. SARS may reopen VAT assessments within 5 years from the date of the relevant return (TAA s.99(3)); no prescription period applies where fraud, misrepresentation, or non-disclosure is proven. Criminal conviction for fraudulent export zero-rating may result in imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine. Retrospective clawback operates from the time of supply — SARS can recover input VAT previously refunded against the zero-rated export and apply a further 10% per month? (corrected to: applicable additional tax under VAT Act s.59 where a vendor issues incorrect tax invoices) plus penalities and interest until full recovery. In practice, SARS conducts heightened audit scrutiny of cotton and agricultural commodity exporters in SACU corridors, including cross-matching SAD 500 releases, SARB exchange-control records, and the 180-day repatriation deadline.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The South African Rand (ZAR) is fully and freely convertible for current account transactions, including export proceeds, trade settlement, dividends, interest, and service fees, subject to an authorised dealer obtaining customary supporting documentation. Capital account transactions remain subject to exchange control; inward foreign equity investment into a South African (Pty) Ltd requires no prior SARB approval when implemented via ordinary banking channels and recorded as an inward foreign investment, whereas outward transfers and foreign loans (shareholder loans) require FinSurv approval or use of the assisted-trade or loan-facility frameworks. Dividend remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted through authorised dealers without SARB approval solely where the dividend is declared out of profits and accompanied by an auditor's certificate confirming the distribution's legal permissibility and that the underlying funds do not represent disallowed capital. Other profit repatriation modes, such as royalty or management fee remittances, require approval or must satisfy the relevant withholding tax and supporting invoice requirements. No significant current-account convertibility hurdle exists for the target cotton-bale export brokerage, but all outbound payments must clear through the national payment system and FX purchased only from authorised dealers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds must be repatriated to South Africa within 180 days from the date of shipment of the ginned raw cotton bales, unless an extension has been granted by FinSurv or the exporter's authorised dealer. There is no fixed statutory percentage mandatory conversion requirement: as of the current SARB policy framework, the mandatory surrender requirement to convert all export receipts into ZAR was abolished; export proceeds may either be settled into ZAR at the exporter's discretion or credited to a Foreign Currency Account (FCA) held with an authorised dealer. Full proceeds of the underlying export portion attributable to the brokerage mandate must be accounted for; shortfalls require documented evidence of bank charges, agent commissions, or approved retention. Retention balances in FCA corporate accounts above the authorised dealer's discretion threshold (typically higher-tier corporate balances) require FinSurv approval. Exporters must also ensure the export is underpinned by a valid invoice, customs export entry (SAD500 form), and any required cotton-export licensing documentation, so that the authorised dealer can verify the genuine trade cover before crediting FCA or effecting conversion.

Central bank supervision: South African Reserve Bank (SARB), acting through the Financial Surveillance Department (FinSurv), pursuant to the Currency and Exchanges Act, 1933 (Act No. 9 of 1933), the Exchange Control Regulations promulgated thereunder, and the SARB Currency and Exchanges Manual (amended as at 1 January 2021 with subsequent notice updates). Operational exchange control is delegated to Authorised Dealers (i.e., domestic banks with Authorised Dealer status) and Authorised Dealers in foreign exchange with limited authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW for non-bank offshore digital EMI-only arrangements. South African exchange control requires all external foreign-currency transactions to flow through Authorised Dealers (i.e., licensed domestic banks or licensed ADs in foreign exchange). Offshore digital EMIs that are not licensed as South African authorised dealers cannot lawfully execute the ZAR leg, originate SWIFT customer transfers on their own balance sheet, or hold the company's FCA; they cannot provide the importer/exporter code coverage, exchange-control declarations, and audit trail required by SARB FinSurv. A hybrid model—offshore EMI used solely as a non-bank payment instruction front-end, with the actual clearing executed via a domestic Authorised Dealer—is structurally permissible but operationally redundant and generally discouraged for the target raw-cotton trade where LC and documentary collection rails are preferred. A viable niche is the use of regulated South African payment fintechs (e.g., authorised PISP or corporate payment processors) that layer onto bank rails, but these remain supplementary and do not replace bank PKI/SWIFT capabilities.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer routing for equity or shareholder-loan capital into the South African (Pty) Ltd: the non-resident investor initiates a SWIFT MT103/MT202 instruction to the company's blocked escrow or operational account at the nominated Authorised Dealer. The bank debit/credit memo must be annotated with the correct South African exchange-control purpose code (e.g., 'Equity Investment' / 'Foreign Investment' / code 431 for share capital). For shareholder loans, the pipeline utilises the SARB loan policy: a foreign loan to a resident company must be subjected to prior SARB approval unless within the general approval exemption for inter-company loans up to R20 million with minimum maturity and at arm's-length interest, or the shareholder chooses equity in lieu of loan. The pay-in must be accompanied by the executed share subscription agreement, board resolution allotting shares, CIPC change-of-directors/shareholders filing (if new foreign shareholder), FICA virtue of the ultimate beneficiary, source-of-funds evidence, and the bank's foreign-exchange purchase confirmation. The authorised dealer will issue an inward-transfer confirmation and endorse the company's foreign-liability register, thereby rendering the capital import 'approved' under the exchange-control umbrella and allowing subsequent unrestricted dividend and capital-repatriation entitlements.Trade-settlement clearing rails for the cotton-bale export brokerage are optimised around SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers for commission/price settlements and MT700/MT720-issued commercial letters of credit (sight or usance) or MT750/MT752 documentary-collection instructions where the trade is title-passing. For documentary collections, the flow is: buyer bank (advising/remitting) to the South African exporting bank, with the bill of exchange and bill of lading released on payment (DP) or acceptance (DA); proceeds are credited in the foreign currency to the company's FCA and settled to ZAR as desired. Direct SWIFT MT103 payments in USD/GBP/EUR are credited to the FCA under a narrative referencing the invoice and cotton crop; all outward disbursements (freight, inspection, port handling, buyer rebates) are executed as MT103s with proper current-account narrative, submitted invoices, and tax invoices for VAT input recovery where applicable; the bank clears via SAMOS (South African Multiple Option Settlement) for the ZAR leg and via global nostro relationships for the foreign-currency leg.Onshore plain-vanilla forward exchange contracts (FECs) against firm future export invoices, concluded with a South African Authorised Dealer for a tenor not exceeding 12 months and supported by documentary evidence of the underlying cotton-bale sale; contract cover may be taken on shipment-date basis or fixed-date basis.Covered put/call currency options (e.g., ZAR/USD AUD and CAD) purchased from an authorised dealer, with premium paid upfront in ZAR or foreign currency; writing naked options is prohibited without FinSurv approval.Cross-currency swaps (CCS) between the foreign-currency receivable portfolio and ZAR, executed with an authorised dealer to hedge medium-term working-capital exposure; CCS is permitted for firm commitments only.Onshore non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are NOT generally permissible for resident companies with genuine onshore ZAR convertibility exposure, as the SARB maintains that residents must use deliverable forwards through authorised dealers; however, non-resident counterparties may utilise offshore NDFs in conjunction with a non-deliverable hedging strategy, and, under specific FinSurv exemptions, a resident company with no ZAR conversion capability may apply for NDF participation. The target company should instead rely on deliverable FECs.Currency interest-rate swaps (CIRS) and basis swaps where the debt leg is a foreign shareholder loan and the ZAR/foreign-currency risk must be synthetically reduced, subject to the underlying loan being exchange-control approved.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: LOW for non-bank offshore digital EMI-only arrangements. South African exchange control requires all external foreign-currency transactions to flow through Authorised Dealers (i.e., licensed domestic banks or licensed ADs in foreign exchange). Offshore digital EMIs that are not licensed as South African authorised dealers cannot lawfully execute the ZAR leg, originate SWIFT customer transfers on their own balance sheet, or hold the company's FCA; they cannot provide the importer/exporter code coverage, exchange-control declarations, and audit trail required by SARB FinSurv. A hybrid model—offshore EMI used solely as a non-bank payment instruction front-end, with the actual clearing executed via a domestic Authorised Dealer—is structurally permissible but operationally redundant and generally discouraged for the target raw-cotton trade where LC and documentary collection rails are preferred. A viable niche is the use of regulated South African payment fintechs (e.g., authorised PISP or corporate payment processors) that layer onto bank rails, but these remain supplementary and do not replace bank PKI/SWIFT capabilities.
